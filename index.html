The Off-Policy Admissibility of KL Divergence: An Empirical Study
This section reports a controlled study, run inside the ③ Admissibility lab, isolating the single property that distinguishes KL divergence among general strictly-convex policy regularizers: its implicit-reward inner term is independent of the policy, and therefore requires no estimation from action samples. All numbers below were produced by the same tabular trajectory-DPO learner used in the interactive panel.

1. Setup

We study a layered Markov decision process with L decision layers, three states per layer, and three actions. Taking action ai moves to the next-layer state si with probability 1−ε and spreads uniformly over the remaining states otherwise; we use ε = 0.2 throughout. Rewards are drawn once per instance and held fixed. For each regularizer Ω we obtain its regularized-optimal policy πΩ★ in closed form by backward induction, using the exact stationarity solver for each divergence (softmax for KL, a one-dimensional bisection on the inverse of ∇Ω for the remaining smooth divergences, and Euclidean projection for χ² and squared Euclidean).

To make the seven regularizers comparable rather than confounded by the scale of Ω, we calibrate the regularization weight α per divergence so that every πΩ★ attains the same peakedness — the mean over states of its largest action probability. Fixing a target peak of 0.6 and solving for α by bisection yields the values in Table 1; the calibration is exact to numerical tolerance and can be reproduced live in the α–peak sweep panel.

Preference data are generated off-policy: trajectory pairs are rolled out under a uniform behavior policy, and a Bradley–Terry label is sampled from the difference of their discounted returns. A single dataset of N = 4000 pairs is shared verbatim by all seven learners, so the only quantity that can differ across divergences is the treatment of the implicit-reward inner term inside the learner.

2. The inner term and its estimation

Writing the regularized optimal action-value through the Legendre map gives an implicit reward of the form r̂(s,a) = α[∇Ω(πθ)(a) − ∇Ω(πref)(a)] + α CΩ(s′), where the inner term CΩ(s′) = 𝔼a′∼π(·|s′)[Φ] is an expectation over next-state actions. For KL this term is a state-independent constant and cancels in every preference comparison; for any other regularizer it depends on the policy and must be estimated from nmc sampled actions. Table 2 quantifies this at the calibrated optima: KL has exactly zero across-state variation and zero single-sample estimator noise, whereas every other divergence carries both.

3. The admissibility gap

We train each divergence with tabular trajectory-DPO (Adam, learning rate 0.08, 300 steps, minibatch 16), estimating CΩ with nmc on-policy samples and differentiating through the estimator exactly. Performance is the mean total-variation distance between the trained policy and that divergence's own optimum, averaged over states, so each method is scored against its own target on a common ruler. We report mean ± standard deviation over eight seeds.

Three observations follow. First, at nmc=1 — the regime in which the learner has the least information about the inner expectation — KL attains a gap of 0.106, while every other divergence lands between 0.17 and 0.21, a separation of four to seven standard deviations. Second, KL is essentially flat in nmc (0.106 → 0.100 from one to sixteen samples), exactly as predicted by the constancy of its inner term, whereas every other method improves monotonically as more samples reduce estimator variance. Third, even at nmc=16 a residual ordering persists, with KL still lowest; the non-KL methods approach but do not reach it within this sample budget.

It is worth isolating the effect from confounds. Because the dataset, the MDP, the optimizer, and the calibration are shared, the only moving part across columns is inner-term estimation. We therefore read the excess gap over KL at nmc=1, which removes the common floor due to finite data and optimization residual: α-div +0.084, Reverse KL +0.103, JS +0.077, Hellinger +0.068, χ² +0.092, Euclidean +0.076. The penalty tracks the estimator noise of Table 2 in rank, with Reverse KL and χ² — the divergences with the largest inner-term variance — incurring the largest excess.

4. The penalty accumulates along trajectories

If the gap originates in the inner term, which appears once per transition, it should grow with trajectory length. Holding all else fixed and sweeping the horizon L, we find the mean excess gap over KL (averaged across the six non-KL divergences, nmc=1) increases from 0.031 at L=3 to 0.056 at L=4 and 0.095 at L=5, while KL's own gap stays near 0.10 throughout. Longer horizons accumulate more inner-term estimation error for the non-KL methods, sharpening KL's advantage; KL is unaffected because there is no error to accumulate.

Figure 4. Effect of trajectory depth L. KL’s own gap (pink) stays near 0.10 across depths, while the mean excess of the non-KL divergences over KL (blue) grows with L, consistent with the inner-term error being incurred once per transition and accumulating along the trajectory.

5. The extreme off-policy regime

The on-policy estimator above still resamples fresh actions from the current policy at each visited next state. A strictly off-policy learner has no such luxury: the dataset records exactly one action at each state, and no further environment queries are available. Evaluating the inner term at the single data-recorded action — the most faithful rendering of the off-policy constraint — replaces estimation variance with estimation bias, since the behavior policy is not the target policy. Under this protocol KL is unchanged at 0.106±0.016, identical to its on-policy value, while the non-KL divergences remain elevated at 0.16–0.20. KL is the only divergence whose performance is invariant to the inner-term protocol, because it never evaluates the inner term at all.

Figure 5. Admissibility of the c(s′) degree of freedom. A non-uniform within-layer weight (values {0.6, 1.0, 1.6}) is attached to KL two ways. When c depends on the next state (green) the inner term is recoverable from the logged transition and the violation is identically zero for all ε. When c depends on the next action (purple) the two indexings coincide at ε=0 — where the action is pinned by the state — and the violation grows as transition noise makes the action unrecoverable from the state. The free scalar is harmless precisely when it respects the (s,a,s′)-measurability that defines admissibility.

5.5 The c(s′) degree of freedom is admissibility-neutral

Theorem 2 characterizes the admissible regularizers up to a per-state positive scalar c(s′) multiplying the entire regularizer. A natural worry is whether this freedom is a loophole through which non-admissible behavior could re-enter. It is not, and the reason is exactly the measurability condition (A4): the inner term remains a function of s′ alone, so it is still computable from a logged transition. We make this operational by attaching a non-uniform within-layer weight (configurable; default values {0.6, 1.0, 1.6}) to KL and asking how much of the inner term cannot be reconstructed from (s, a, s′) — the admissibility violation — as a function of transition noise.

Indexing the weight by the next state, c(s′), leaves the violation identically zero at every ε: the weight is part of the logged transition and nothing must be estimated. Indexing instead by the next action, c(a′), is a deliberate violation of (A4) — yet at ε = 0 it is indistinguishable from state-indexing, because a deterministic transition makes the action a bijective function of the next state and therefore recoverable. As ε increases the action is progressively unrecoverable from the state, and the violation rises monotonically from zero (Figure 5). We define the violation as the root-mean-square inner-term residual left after conditioning on the logged transition, √(𝔼s′ Vara′|s′[c]), where the posterior P(a′|s′) follows from the behavior policy and the ε-transition kernel by Bayes' rule; it is zero exactly when c is measurable with respect to s′. The boundary case is itself informative: it shows that admissibility is not about the syntactic form of c but about whether c is measurable with respect to the logged transition, with the action becoming an admissible index exactly when it carries no information beyond the state.

6. Discussion

Across calibration, estimator statistics, the nmc sweep, the horizon sweep, and the extreme off-policy protocol, a single pattern recurs: KL incurs no inner-term cost, and every other strictly-convex regularizer incurs a cost that grows with estimator variance, with trajectory length, and under tighter off-policy constraints. This is the empirical signature of off-policy admissibility — the property that the implicit reward can be evaluated from logged (s,a,s′) transitions alone — and the experiments isolate it as the operative distinction, rather than any difference in expressivity or optimization difficulty, all of which were held fixed by construction.
